## Slide 13 — What is Solid

**Layout:** Two columns — text left, Pod diagram + Solid logo right.

- **Heading:** What is Solid
- **Body:**
  - Solid is an open-source **project**, open **standard**, and **movement** for a **user-centric data** storage system, originating from Sir Tim Berners-Lee's lab at MIT CSAIL.
  - Your data lives in a **Pod**: a personal or organisational datastore with fine-grained access controls. Apps read and write only **with permission**, **breaking down data silos** and keeping data under the control of whoever it describes.
  - The ODI stewards Solid's standards, codebases, and communities.
- **Imagery:** "Solid Pods" network diagram + Solid logo.

**Notes (Jesse, ~1.5 min):** I'm Jesse Wright, here with Matthieu Bosquet — we both work on Solid at the ODI. One sentence: Solid is an open standard for user-centric data and identity on the web. Today every app keeps its own copy of your data; Solid inverts that. Your data lives in a Pod, and apps — and, as you'll see, AI agents — read and write only with your permission, at the level of individual items. Open linked-data standards mean the same data is reused everywhere without custom integration, always under the control of whoever it describes. Privacy by design, built on standard web tech from Tim's group at MIT, stewarded by the ODI, standardised at the W3C.

## Slide 16 — The standard / protocol stack

**Layout:** Full-width diagram of the web protocol stack with Solid as the top layer.

- **Heading:** What is Solid (client-server specs)
- **Diagram content:** Bottom-up stack — physical (Ethernet, WiFi, Mobile data, Fibre) → IP v4/v6 → TCP → DNS → URI → HTTP → HTML → JS/ECMAScript → Web APIs → **Solid layer: Universal Data API (LDP) · AuthN (global SSO) · AuthZ**. Era markers: Web 1.0 (HTTP/HTML/URL), Web 2.0 (JavaScript/Web APIs), Web 3.0 (Global SSO, AuthZ, Universal API).

**Notes (Matthieu, ~1 min):** Where this sits technically: Web 1.0 gave us HTTP, HTML and URLs — the document web. Web 2.0 added JavaScript and web APIs — the interactive web. Solid is the next layer, adding the three things the web never standardised: a global single sign-on so you have one identity everywhere, fine-grained authorization so you control access per resource, and a universal data API so any app can read and write your data in a standard way. All open W3C standards — no lock-in. Rather than describe it, let's show you.

## Slide 23 — FIBO & financial services

**Layout:** Left: FIBO↔Solid "interop" diagram + caption. Right: three use-case blocks.

- **Kicker:** Elisa Kendall · FIBO & financial services
- **Heading:** FIBO is the bridge between finance and Solid
- **Diagram:** FIBO (shared meaning) ── interop ── Solid (user control).
- **Diagram caption:** Bind FIBO to Solid and data in a Pod means the same thing to every institution: **semantic interoperability**, nothing new invented.
- **Use-case blocks:**
  - **Lending on verified, current data** — Agreements modelled in FIBO, verified against the borrower's Pod, read at source, not re-keyed.
  - **Reusable KYC / AML** — Identity, address and source-of-funds proofed once, confirmed by signature check: weeks and cost out of onboarding.
  - **Bank-provided identity** — Banks issue credentials into a Pod the customer presents elsewhere: a cost centre becomes a service line (see NatWest's Solid pilots).

**Notes (Elisa, ~5 min):** I'm Elisa Kendall; I co-lead the FIBO and ontology work. FIBO — the Financial Industry Business Ontology — is years of work by the financial community to agree, machine-readably, what terms mean: a loan, a legal entity, a counterparty. Finance's problem isn't a shortage of data; it's that the same concept means subtly different things in every system, so data can't move without expensive reconciliation. Solid gives you the place to put data under the subject's control and a standard way to read it — but control without shared meaning isn't enough; two banks reading the same Pod still need to agree what the fields mean. FIBO supplies that meaning. Bind FIBO to Solid and you get semantic interoperability — and we bind an ontology finance already trusts rather than asking anyone to adopt something new. Three use cases: lending on data verified against the borrower's Pod; KYC/AML where identity is proven once and reused by signature check; and bank-provided identity, where a bank issues credentials into the customer's Pod — turning a compliance cost centre into a service line. NatWest has already piloted exactly this. Finance leads because EDMA's roots and FIBO are here, and the cost of the status quo is enormous. Stuart will take it to the bottom line.

## Slide 24 — Business value

**Layout:** Six coloured-tick blocks (3×2 grid).

- **Kicker:** Stuart Coleman · business value
- **Heading:** Cost out, risk down, new revenue in
- **Blocks:**
  1. **Less data custody** — Read from the Pod instead of holding copies: lower cost, liability and compliance burden.
  2. **Reusable credentials** — Proven identity and eligibility reused across sectors; onboarding and KYC/AML cost falls.
  3. **Single source of truth** — Update once, not everywhere: less reconciliation, fewer stale-copy errors.
  4. **Interoperability, no lock-in** — Open W3C-track standard; shared sector ontologies, not bespoke integrations.
  5. **New permissioned revenue** — Value-added services, like bank-provided identity, on user-controlled data.
  6. **AI-ready & future-proof** — Consented, attributable data for trustworthy AI; aligns with GDPR Art. 20, the EU Data Act and DMA.

**Notes (Stuart, ~5 min):** Back to me for the bottom line. Elisa gave the how; here's why it pays, in six ways. One: less data custody — read from the Pod with permission instead of securing your own copy; every copy you don't hold is a breach you can't have. Two: reusable credentials proven once and reused across services and sectors — time and money out of onboarding and KYC/AML. Three: a single current source of truth — the customer updates once, so less reconciliation and fewer stale-data decisions. Four: interoperability without lock-in — an open W3C standard, interoperability from shared ontologies this community defines, not another bespoke integration or a platform you can't leave. Five — cost to growth — new permissioned services and revenue, like bank-provided identity turning a cost centre into a product. Six: future-proofing — structured, consented, attributable data for trustworthy AI and agents, and demonstrable user control that aligns with GDPR Article 20, the EU Data Act and the DMA. Cost out, risk down, new revenue in — while doing right by customers. The way to capture it is to be in the room while the rails are laid.
